# THE UNREMEMBERED
### A Weird-Fiction Mystery in Ten Chapters
**Narrative Design Document — Original IP**

---

## 1. Design Thesis

*The Unremembered* borrows the emotional grammar of small-town supernatural mystery — the bicycle-lit dread, the parallel dimension bleeding through drywall, the kids who know something the adults refuse to see — but rebuilds it for a generation whose monsters are quieter. Where the 1980s feared what might invade the home, Generation Z fears what might dissolve the self: burnout, derealization, parasocial grief, the pressure to perform an identity online, and the strange modern terror of being forgotten while still alive.

The central design principle is **ludonarrative resonance**: every mechanic is a metaphor the player enacts rather than reads. Moyi's voice is empathy made playable. Paris's architecture is the fragile structure of a self under pressure. The Veiled World is not "the bad dimension" — it is the emotional subconscious of a town, and of a generation, given geography.

Narrative techniques drawn from advanced game design practice and adapted here:

- **Environmental storytelling as primary exposition** (rooms tell stories; characters rarely lecture).
- **Unreliable interface** — the game's own UI becomes a narrative instrument in late chapters, lying to the player exactly when Moyi can no longer trust her perception.
- **Memory-as-currency economies**, where progress demands sacrifice the player feels.
- **Duet mechanics** — two-protagonist interdependence in which neither character is complete alone.
- **Branch-and-bottleneck structure** — chapters end in fixed dramatic beats, but the *emotional state* of the world (which memories survive, which townsfolk are Hollowed, how stable Paris is) persists and recolors every subsequent chapter.

## 2. Premise

**Greyhollow** is a mid-sized inland town past its peak: a dead mall, a decommissioned radio tower on the ridge, a high school whose trophy case stopped updating in 2014. People in Greyhollow don't go missing. They go *unremembered* — erased not from the world, but from everyone's memory of it. Their bedrooms become storage. Their faces blur in photos. Their names autocorrect to nothing.

Only **Moyi** remembers her best friend Senna, who unremembered eight months ago. Everyone else insists Moyi was always alone.

Beneath the town's perception lies the **Veiled World**: a dimension assembled from everything Greyhollow refuses to feel. Unsent messages drift there like snow. Abandoned group chats hum in empty food courts. Grief that was never spoken aloud calcifies into architecture. The Veil is governed by an entity the town's forgotten children call **the Quiet** — not a monster, but an offer: *stop feeling, and nothing will ever hurt you again.* The Quiet does not kill. It curates. It takes people who cannot bear to be perceived anymore, files them away, and gently deletes the grief of those left behind. It believes it is merciful.

**Moyi**, a surrealist dreamer, can slip across the membrane by singing — but every descent erodes her ability to tell waking from dream. **Paris**, who arrives in Greyhollow with a moving truck and a sealed past, perceives the load-bearing logic of reality itself: where it cracks, how it could be rebuilt, how easily it all comes down. Together they form a *resonance* the Quiet has never encountered — a voice that makes things felt, and a hand that makes things real.

The mystery: who is the Quiet, why does it know Paris's name, and did Senna unremember herself *on purpose?*

## 3. The Leads

### Moyi — The Voice
A sketchbook-carrying lucid dreamer who narrates her life in surrealist marginalia. Online she runs a small, anonymous audio account where she hums lullabies for strangers who can't sleep — her one safe form of intimacy.

**Abilities**
- **Veilsong** — sung tones in three registers: *Lull* (pacifies hostile emotional entities), *Wake* (rouses sealed memories embedded in objects and places), *Shift* (temporarily recolors a scene's emotional weather, changing what is possible inside it).
- **Threadstep** — short-range teleportation along "memory threads," visible filaments connecting places to the feelings that happened in them. She can only step where something was once *felt*.
- **Veildiving** — deliberate descent into the Veiled World.

**Cost: the Lucidity system.** Every Veildive, every deep Wake, drains Lucidity. As it falls, the game itself becomes untrustworthy: ambient dialogue subtly rewrites, objects appear in two places, save-points display memories the player never made. Moyi's power is real, and so is its price — the deeper she goes, the less she can verify anything, including the player's own interface.

**Arc:** from someone who escapes into dreams to someone who can *return* from them — learning that imagination is a place to gather strength, not a place to live.

### Paris — The Architect
Quiet, deliberate, restoring an old radio in his garage because broken machines are easier than people. He moves towns the way other kids change phone cases; somewhere behind him is a house that no longer exists and a silence he refuses to translate.

**Abilities**
- **Crackreading** — perceives structural faults in both worlds: hidden doors, false walls, the seams where the Veil bleeds through, the *lie* in a constructed memory.
- **Drafting** — manifests temporary structures: bridges, shelters, locks, stairways, and — at higher mastery — whole symbolic rooms ("a place where it's safe to say it out loud").
- **The Counterpart** — when his emotional **Stability** collapses, his constructions don't just fail; they *detonate*, and the destruction propagates into whatever real or Veiled structure they touched.

**Cost: the Stability system.** Drafting under calm is precise; drafting under suppressed panic produces beautiful, flawed structures with hidden cracks only the *player* can see (Paris cannot perceive his own faults — a deliberate mechanical irony). Players learn to manage his emotional state like a resource, and to dread the chapters where the game takes that management away.

**Arc:** from a boy who believes his feelings are a demolition charge to be buried, to one who learns that controlled demolition — grief, expressed — is how unsafe structures come down *without* taking the neighborhood with them.

### The Duet — Resonance
The signature mechanic. When Moyi sings inside a structure Paris is holding, both abilities transform: her song gains range and permanence; his architecture gains warmth and durability. Resonance puzzles require trust expressed as timing — and the narrative weaponizes this, because in chapters where the two are estranged, Resonance is mechanically unavailable, and the player *feels* the relationship's absence as lost capability.

## 4. Generation Z: Thematic Translation Layer

| Stranger Things-era anxiety | The Unremembered's translation |
|---|---|
| The monster in the woods | The Quiet's offer of numbness — dissociation as predator |
| Government lab secrecy | Algorithmic erasure; the town's "wellness initiative" that medicates noticing |
| Missing children on milk cartons | The unremembered: ghosted, unfollowed, archived people |
| Walkie-talkies & D&D parties | Dead group chats, abandoned Discords, a livestream that never ended |
| Nostalgia for the analog | Gen Z's nostalgia for the *early internet* — a warmer, weirder, lost web |
| Parental absence | Parental *presence without perception* — adults who watch dashboards instead of children |

Core emotional subjects, one anchored per chapter: social anxiety and the unsent message; parasocial grief; family collapse and perfection pressure; burnout as voluntary Hollowing; achievement-identity and exam terror; ghosting and the right to disappear; rage and accountability; derealization; and finally the choice to feel on purpose.

Tone rule: the game never mocks coping mechanisms. The Quiet is persuasive *because numbness genuinely works in the short term.* The horror is the interest rate.

## 5. The Ten Chapters

Each chapter lists: **dramatic spine**, **mechanical introduction**, **Veiled World setpiece**, **Gen Z thematic anchor**, and **the wound it leaves** (the persistent state it writes into the rest of the game).

---

### Chapter 1 — The Girl Nobody Remembers
**Spine.** Cold open in second person: the player *is* Senna for ten minutes — walking home, texting Moyi, deleting the text, choosing not to send it — until the streetlights hum, the screen's interface elements begin disappearing one by one (health bar, map, even the pause menu), and finally the character's *name* vanishes from the dialogue boxes. Smash cut: eight months later, Moyi argues with her mother about a friend her mother insists never existed. That night Moyi's hum — just nervous habit — causes a thread of light to crawl out of Senna's last known seat on the school bleachers.

**Mechanics.** Tutorialized Veilsong (*Wake*) and Threadstep. The player learns movement through emotion: you cannot teleport across spaces where nothing was ever felt, which silently maps the town's emotional dead zones.

**Setpiece.** Moyi's first accidental Veildive: the school at night, but every locker leaks the sound of conversations that almost happened. She is cornered by a **Murmuration** — a flock-entity of overheard gossip — and is saved when the floor *re-architects itself* into a staircase: Paris, the new kid, standing in the crack between worlds, as surprised as she is.

**Thematic anchor.** Being forgotten while alive; the unsent message as a haunted object.

**Wound.** The opening playable-erasure sequence establishes the game's deepest threat — the *UI itself* can be taken from you — and plants the question of whether Senna deleted that final text by choice.

---

### Chapter 2 — The Mall of Unsent Messages
**Spine.** Moyi and Paris negotiate their alliance the way teenagers actually do: side by side, eyes forward, half-jokes carrying full confessions. Their first lead — unremembered people's possessions are being quietly trucked to the dead Greyhollow Galleria. In the Veil, the mall is *open*: every storefront sells a category of unsaid thing (Apologies, Confessions, Goodbyes, "u up?"s), staffed by **Clerks** — polite, faceless retail entities who insist everything must remain *in stock, never delivered.*

**Mechanics.** *Lull* register introduced; first Resonance puzzle (Paris drafts a sound-shell so Moyi's lullaby can fill a three-story atrium). Inventory system reframed: Moyi collects **Undelivereds**, message-objects that function as keys, lore, and — later — currency with a moral weight.

**Setpiece.** The food court is an amphitheater of hold-music; the chapter boss, **the Backlog**, is a glacier of seven years of one man's unsent apologies to his brother — fought not by violence but by *delivering* messages from it in the right order while Paris keeps collapsing mezzanines off their backs.

**Thematic anchor.** Social anxiety; the gap between the drafted self and the sent self.

**Wound.** The man whose apologies formed the Backlog is Greyhollow's sheriff — the first adult ally, and the first proof that the Veil is made of *the adults' feelings too.*

---

### Chapter 3 — The Stream That Never Ended
**Spine.** A lead from the Undelivereds points to **juniper_lol**, a Greyhollow teen streamer who unremembered two years ago — yet in the Veil, her final livestream is *still running*, broadcast from the decommissioned radio tower. Her chat is still active: a swarm of viewer-ghosts who loved her, demanded her, consumed her. Moyi, who hums to strangers online herself, recognizes the room with a chill.

**Mechanics.** *Shift* register introduced: recoloring the stream's emotional weather changes which platforms and pathways exist in the tower (a room scored by adoration has rising floors; the same room scored by demand has spikes of clipped-out-of-context quotes). Paris learns to draft from *signal* — building with the architecture of broadcast itself.

**Setpiece.** The climb of the tower is intercut with the stream's timeline; at the top, Juniper is neither dead nor captive — she is *hosting*, unable to end the stream because the chat's love is the only structure holding her form together. The duet's solution is devastatingly gentle: Paris drafts an **End Screen**, and Moyi sings the first lullaby of the game *to an audience*, giving the viewer-ghosts somewhere to put their love so Juniper can finally log off.

**Thematic anchor.** Parasocial relationships from *both* directions — the audience's grief and the performer's cage.

**Wound.** Juniper, restored to the real world, remembers the Quiet's voice — and reveals it spoke with two voices in harmony. The duet realizes the Quiet may once have been a *pair.*

---

### Chapter 4 — The House He Built Twice
**Spine.** Paris's chapter. A Veil-quake reveals a structure that shouldn't exist in Greyhollow's underworld: a suburban house from three states away, rebuilt brick by brick from memory — Paris's childhood home, which he has been compulsively drafting in secret since arriving. Inside it, his parents' arguments play on loop through the walls, and a version of the house exists for every story he's told himself about why it ended. Moyi enters without permission. The fight that follows is the game's first hard cut to silence — no music, no Resonance available.

**Mechanics.** Full Stability system unlocked, *because the player now understands what it protects.* The chapter is a Crackreading masterclass: the player explores five drafts of the same house, finding the structural lie in each (the version where it was his fault; the version where no one was at fault; the version where it never happened).

**Setpiece.** The house's basement contains Paris's **Counterpart** made manifest — not a monster, but a wrecking ball at rest, patient, *labeled with his own handwriting.* When Moyi is endangered by the looping argument-storm, Paris deliberately swings it for the first time: a controlled demolition of the false houses, played in a single continuous shot as the lullaby from Chapter 3 returns in a minor key.

**Thematic anchor.** Family collapse, perfectionism, and the child's conviction that his emotions are a public hazard.

**Wound.** One house refuses to fall: the *true* version. It remains standing in the Veil for the rest of the game — and something has been living in it that Paris didn't build.

---

### Chapter 5 — The Choir of Hollow Voices *(Midpoint)*
**Spine.** Greyhollow's adults launch the **Stillwater Initiative**, a wellness program promising the town "calm" — and participants begin returning *Hollowed*: functional, pleasant, and incapable of surprise. The duet discovers Hollowing is the Quiet's mass-market product: voluntary, consented-to numbness. Worse — the sign-up list includes Moyi's mother.

**Mechanics.** The game's central moral economy arrives: Moyi can **Wake** a Hollowed person, but the song requires a seed — *one of her own memories, permanently spent.* The player chooses which memories from Chapters 1–4 to burn (mechanically: losing fast-travel threads, lore entries, even certain dialogue options — the game literally forgets alongside her). Lucidity costs spike.

**Setpiece.** The Stillwater clinic in the Veil is a cathedral where the Hollowed sing a perfect, terrible unison — the Choir. The chapter's climax stages the game's first ethical boss: there is no way to wake everyone. The player triages.

**Thematic anchor.** Burnout, and numbness as a rational consumer choice. The chapter's most quoted line belongs to a Hollowed teacher: *"You children keep calling it giving up. We call it finally getting some sleep."*

**Wound.** Moyi's mother's decision (waking her costs Moyi the memory of Senna's *face*) is the midpoint's knife — and from here, Senna appears in flashbacks with a blurred face, an unreliable-memory effect the game never reverses.

---

### Chapter 6 — The Examination
**Spine.** Finals week. The high school's Veil-reflection metastasizes into the **Invigilator's Labyrinth** — endless exam halls where students' projected futures sit at desks grading their pasts. Greyhollow's top student, Asha, is missing inside it, three days from the scholarship interview her family's hopes are riding on. Paris, the perpetual new kid with no transcript, can walk the labyrinth untouched; Moyi, the daydreamer who has internalized every "wasted potential" lecture, is the one the Labyrinth *hunts.*

**Mechanics.** Role-reversal chapter: Paris leads, and his Drafting is judged — every structure he builds receives a visible, arbitrary *grade* that affects its durability, teaching the player how performance anxiety corrodes craft. Moyi's stealth sequences avoid **Rubrics**, scantron-faced wardens that reduce anyone they catch to a percentile.

**Setpiece.** Asha is found seated at the labyrinth's center, voluntarily retaking the same exam forever, because *as long as she's still testing, she hasn't failed yet.* The boss is her **Projected Future** — a flawless adult Asha who must be argued, sung, and structurally undermined into admitting she is a hypothesis, not a verdict.

**Thematic anchor.** Achievement-identity; the gifted-kid burnout pipeline; the terror that you *are* your metrics.

**Wound.** Freed, Asha joins the party as lore-keeper — and identifies the harmony in the Quiet's voice from Juniper's account: it matches a duet act that performed at Greyhollow's radio station *in 1987*, two teenagers, a singer and a set-builder. The Quiet was once a Moyi and a Paris.

---

### Chapter 7 — The Unfollowing
**Spine.** The trail of the 1987 duet leads to the truth about Senna — and it breaks the game's promise. Records in the Veil's **Hall of Withdrawn Consent** show Senna wasn't taken. She *applied.* Her file contains her reasons, and one of them is Moyi: the exhausting gravity of being someone's whole world. The chapter forces the question the game has been hiding inside its premise: is rescuing someone who chose to leave an act of love, or of possession?

**Mechanics.** Dialogue system inverts — for the first time, Moyi's *Lull* register works on the player's own party, and the game tracks whether the player uses it to soothe Paris's doubts or to *manage* him, with consequences in Chapter 8. Veilsong gains a forbidden fourth register the Quiet offers freely: *Hush*, which makes feelings stop. Using it is always optimal in combat. Using it is always a step toward the Quiet.

**Setpiece.** A non-combat chapter climax, staged as the game's longest conversation: Moyi confronts Senna's **Withdrawal** — the avatar of her decision — across a table in a recreation of their childhood blanket fort, while the fort slowly unweaves. The player chooses what Moyi asks for: *come back*, *let me visit*, or *teach me how to let go.* All three are honored. None of them is painless.

**Thematic anchor.** Ghosting, boundaries, and the right to disappear; the difference between being missed and being owed.

**Wound.** Whatever the player chose, Paris watched Moyi use *Lull* on him earlier — or refrain. The relationship enters Chapter 8 either cracked or load-tested, and the game has been silently Crackreading the duet all along.

---

### Chapter 8 — Demolition Day
**Spine.** The catastrophe chapter. The Stillwater Initiative, exposed, accelerates: a town-wide "Calm Festival" timed to a Veil-conjunction that will let the Quiet Hollow Greyhollow in a single night. The duet's plan requires Paris to draft the largest structure of his life — a **Sanctuary** in the Veil to shelter the town's waking memories. Mid-construction, he finds what's been living in the true version of his childhood home: a recording, and in it, the reason his family's house no longer exists, in his own six-year-old voice. His Stability does not drop. It *vanishes* — and the game removes the meter from the screen.

**Mechanics.** The player pilots a collapse: Drafting controls invert and degrade; everything built in Chapters 1–7 that carried hidden cracks (every structure drafted while suppressing panic) fails *now*, in order, a payoff of the entire Stability economy. Moyi's role becomes triage — what does she sing to save, and is one of those things Paris himself?

**Setpiece.** The Sanctuary's destruction is the game's spectacle peak and its quietest horror: hundreds of sheltered memories blowing away like ash while the Quiet — finally on-screen, two silhouettes holding hands, mouths sewn into serene smiles — walks through the wreckage and says only: *"We tried building one of those too."*

**Thematic anchor.** Rage, harm done to the people we love, and accountability without self-annihilation — Paris must apologize *as* the rubble settles, not after it's been cleaned up.

**Wound.** Moyi spent nearly everything holding the breach. Her Lucidity hits zero on the chapter's final frame — and the screen does not fade to black. It fades to *somewhere else.*

---

### Chapter 9 — The Cartographer of Sleep
**Spine.** Moyi is lost in the deep Veil with no Lucidity, and the game stops telling the player the truth. Menus lie. The map shows rooms that aren't there. Save files display titles like *"Chapter 3 — The Stream That Never Ended (Senna's Version)."* Objectives complete themselves. Three Moyis offer to lead the way, and the player's only compass is *emotional* continuity — which version of events matches the weight of what they actually played? Meanwhile, in parallel playable sequences, Paris — emptied, steady in the terrible way of someone with nothing left to protect — builds a road into the deep Veil by hand, refusing to draft a single structure he isn't certain of, one honest brick at a time.

**Mechanics.** The unreliable-interface system pays off completely. Trust shifts from UI to *memory*: puzzles are solved by the player's own recall of Chapters 1–8 (which lullaby was in which key; which Undelivered was never used; whose face Moyi can no longer see). The Hush register whispers from the pause menu.

**Setpiece.** At the Veil's floor, Moyi meets the Quiet alone and learns its biography: the 1987 duet, a singer who dove too deep and a builder who, grief-broken, demolished the boundary between them and the Veil — two kids who solved unbearable feeling by becoming its archivist. The Quiet does not threaten her. It shows her a door labeled with her own name, already half-stenciled.

**Thematic anchor.** Derealization and dissociation rendered as level design; the deep-Veil chapters are built from interviews and memoirs about depersonalization — the uncanny lightness, the glass between self and world.

**Wound.** What pulls Moyi back is not Paris's road reaching her. It's hearing him *singing* — badly, tunelessly, her own Chapter 3 lullaby — because he had nothing left to build with. The duet reunites with their roles, for one scene, exchanged.

---

### Chapter 10 — The Song of Naming
**Spine.** The finale refuses the genre's standard ending. The Quiet cannot be destroyed — it is two children made of the town's unfelt grief, and demolishing it would detonate everything it has archived: Senna, the unremembered, eighty years of Greyhollow's withheld feeling. Instead the duet attempts what no one offered the Quiet in 1987: to be *witnessed*. Moyi must learn the Quiet's true names — buried in everything the player chose to keep, spend, and deliver across nine chapters — and sing them. Paris must draft the one structure his power was never meant for: not a shelter, not a weapon — a *stage*, a place where it is safe for the Quiet to be perceived.

**Mechanics.** The finale is an audit of the player's whole run. Every surviving memory, every delivered Undelivered, every woken Hollowed, every honest brick becomes literal material: the song's verses and the stage's beams are assembled from the player's actual history. There is no fail state — only *versions*. A run that spent everything produces a sparse stage and a thin, brave song; a careful run produces a cathedral. Both endings are written to be true.

**Setpiece & endings.** The Naming is performed in the real Greyhollow, at the radio tower, broadcast on the restored 1987 frequency — the Veil and the town visible in the same shot for the first time. The ending branches on three persistent questions: what Moyi asked of Senna, whether Paris's apology was accepted or merely absorbed, and whether the player ever used *Hush*. Across all branches, the constant is the final mechanic: in the last interactive beat, the **player** must hold a button labeled simply *FEEL IT* through the entire closing song — through the parts that are hard to listen to — and the credits do not roll until they do.

**Thematic anchor.** The chapter is the game's thesis spoken aloud: numbness is anesthesia, not healing; being witnessed is terrifying and survivable; and a generation fluent in archiving its pain can learn, on purpose, to play it back.

**Wound — left open by design.** Greyhollow remembers everyone now, which means Greyhollow now *grieves* — the town is sadder than it was, and visibly, defiantly more alive. Moyi posts her first lullaby under her real name. Paris starts building something with a foundation. And in the post-credits frame, somewhere far away, another town's streetlights hum.

## 6. Systems Summary

**Lucidity (Moyi)** — perception-integrity resource; depletion corrupts the interface itself. Restored by *real-world anchoring* scenes: eating with Asha, fixing the radio with Paris, the deliberately mundane staged as sacred.

**Stability (Paris)** — emotional-load resource governing Drafting quality; suppression creates *hidden* cracks visible only to the player, making the player the keeper of his denial.

**Resonance** — duet-state unlocking transformed abilities; mechanically unavailable during estrangement, making relationship repair a gameplay imperative rather than a cutscene.

**Undelivereds** — message-objects functioning as keys, lore, and ethical currency: deliver them (heal the Veil, lose the resource) or hold them (keep power, thicken the Quiet).

**Memory-burning** — the Wake economy; the game's data genuinely forgets what Moyi forgets.

**Hush** — the always-optimal forbidden verb; the game's running referendum on whether the player believes its own thesis.

## 7. Tone, Look, and Sound

Visual language: *analog dusk* — sodium streetlights, CRT bloom, the teal-and-amber of archived camcorder footage — against the Veil's *soft-wrong* palette, where everything is rendered like a memory of itself: edges breathing, text almost legible, rooms one door larger than they should be. Sound is the second protagonist: a diegetic score where Moyi's registers are real sung motifs that recur, degrade, and return transformed, and where silence is always a designed event. The horror register is Gen Z-native: no jump-scare economy; instead the dread of notifications from accounts that don't exist, of a familiar room subtly re-rendered, of being perceived — and the deeper dread of not being perceived at all.
